Always Tea-Time

Three ways to stop the clocks · Saturday the 3rd, four o'clock

Four women who met at eighteen, all turning fifty in the same season, together in a treehouse in the woods.

Monica, we could not have invented a better reason to set a table. You asked for an afternoon where the clocks have stopped and nobody is late, and it turns out that is a thing we very much want to do.

Here is how we would do it, three ways. Everything happens on the lower deck, sixty feet up, with the holler falling away below you and the creek running somewhere under all that green.

Four o'clock, because that is the hour a hungry duchess settled on in 1840 and nobody has improved on it since. Nobody will bring it out to you and nobody will hover. It will simply be waiting when you come down the trail, and the afternoon belongs entirely to the four of you.

The Table

$275

It is always tea-time, and nobody is late.

A round table on the deck with the cloth to the boards, no head and no foot, because a table like this has neither. Every cup different. No saucer belonging to the cup sitting on it. Two teapots that have clearly never met.

Tiered stands loaded with sugar cookies iced as pocket watches, keys, playing cards and teacups, baked in our kitchen that morning.

And here and there among the china, two or three wind-up clocks. All stopped. None of them agreeing with each other.

The tea is blended for the four of you, and the label carries your four names and the date. There is a bag of it for each of you to take home and finish somewhere else, on some ordinary Tuesday in November when you need reminding.

The Garden Gone Over

$495

The party has been going a long while, and the forest has started taking the table back.

Everything above, and then

October flowers, cut local, dark and overgrown rather than sweet, spilling off the edge of the table as though they came up through the boards overnight. A few stems standing in teacups because nobody could find a vase and nobody looked very hard.

A savory tier, so it is a proper meal and not only sugar. And one cake, small, that is wrong in a way we will not be explaining.

Ribbon run through the branches overhead, and a paper lantern or two lit by the bulbs already strung up there.

The Long Afternoon

$850

Time stops for the whole weekend, not for an hour.

Everything above, and then

The morning after. Breakfast crosses the rope bridge to your door as it always does. Except that morning it is cake, because at a table where the clocks have stopped there is no wrong hour to eat cake.

Un-birthdays. Three hundred and sixty-four days a year, not one of you is turning fifty, and that seems worth marking. Something small waits at each place. Four of them. Unwrapped, unexplained, no card. You will know it is coming. They will not.

The photograph. Somewhere in the afternoon, with nobody arranged and nobody posing, we will take a few frames of the four of you at that table. Thirty-odd years of friendship, and we would bet there is not one good photograph of all four of you together anywhere in the world. There will be after this. It arrives the week after you go home, and not one of you has to hold a phone out at arm's length.

The full service rather than whatever fits in two crates. More pots than four people could possibly need. And candles lit on the table in broad daylight, which is a small wrongness that does a great deal of work.

One thing we will not do

No balloons. We are on an open deck above a holler in a conservation forest, and a loose balloon out here is litter with a head start. The ribbon and the lanterns are the better idea anyway, and we mean that rather than saying it.

Tell us which one and we will take it from there. If you want the middle with one thing from the third, or the first with the flowers added, say so. It is your table.

One ask: keep this to yourself. Half of what makes an afternoon like this land is that three of the four did not see it coming.
